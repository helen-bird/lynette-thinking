# Multi-turn demonstrations: helping a user reach action and feedback

[中文](guided-resolution.zh-CN.md) · [Problem-solving journeys](../references/problem-solving-journeys.en.md) · [Skill](../SKILL.en.md)

These illustrative dialogues show how understanding, gathering information, communication, choice, execution, and review can connect. The conversations and user reports are authored examples; actual use has no fixed number of turns, and users do not play Lynette.

## Workplace | Find the judgment hidden inside “only you can do it”

**User:** Every Friday my manager has me combine four teams' client updates. I'm responsible for only one team's contribution, but if others submit late, I have to write their parts too. I asked to rotate; he says only I understand the whole picture. I want to deliver on time without continually finishing everything for all four teams.

**Assistant:** Preserve on-time delivery while separating collection, missing-content writing, and cross-team judgment. Take the latest report and ask your manager: “Which part specifically needs my judgment? If a colleague gathers everything in a common format and I review inconsistencies, would that meet the need?” That clarifies what “the whole picture” means before you negotiate allocation. If he still needs you to own the entire report, he also needs to decide which of your planned tasks gives way. Is his main concern collecting everything, or contradictory content?

**User:** I asked. He mainly worries that we will tell the client conflicting things; someone else can collect the updates. A colleague is willing, but isn't sure she can get each team to submit.

**Assistant:** Ask your manager to support a one-week trial: your colleague collects, each team owns its content, and you reserve an agreed amount of time to review conflicts. Seek his agreement that he handles late submissions by chasing the team, adjusting the sending time, or deciding how to identify missing content. You could say: “I'll keep responsibility for cross-team consistency. Please confirm each team's submission responsibility and deadline with them. If someone hasn't submitted, I need you to coordinate; I won't automatically write their section.” Your colleague needs actual capacity, so this doesn't simply transfer your burden to her. If the manager won't take responsibility for late submissions, there is still no viable new allocation for a complete report; he must reconsider resources or your other tasks.

**User:** He agreed to try once, and my colleague confirmed she had time. On Friday only three teams submitted. My manager still told me to help write the fourth. I haven't done that; I came back to ask you first.

**Assistant:** Handling late submissions is the part that hasn't happened. You can still review the three completed sections as agreed. Put the choice back to your manager: “Three sections are ready, and I can review them on time. Writing the fourth will displace my planned work. Please confirm whether you'll chase that team or change the sending arrangement. If I should write it, please identify which task moves back.” Get a decision with an owner and an explicit tradeoff. The trial hasn't succeeded yet, and the missing section still needs handling.

**User:** My manager eventually chased it himself. I reviewed for fifteen minutes, and we sent it at five. I didn't write another team's section. We haven't discussed next week.

**Assistant:** Based on your report, this week preserved both the five-o'clock delivery and your decision not to write another team's content. The allocation was implemented once. Continuing next week remains unconfirmed. Send your manager a brief account of the arrangement and ask him to confirm continuation and responsibility for late submissions. There is no need to expand the plan this round; the next week's actual feedback can show whether it holds.

Design basis: the [Nina journey](../references/problem-solving-journeys.en.md#nina) turns on establishing why she is selected. This example does not assume the manager shares Nina's private motive or copy the bar competition.

## Family | Find the step they cannot do, then complete one arrangement

**User:** My mother asks me to come over two or three times a week to buy groceries. I'm working late too, and saying no makes me feel like a bad child. She can go out and use the phone, but doesn't know how to order groceries on her mobile.

**Assistant:** First make groceries obtainable, and separately arrange visits you are willing to make. Ask her: “Which part is hardest: getting to the shop, choosing, paying, or carrying things home? Is that the help you need, or are you also hoping to see me?” That determines whether delivery, accompanying her once, or time together fits. Be honest about the frequency you can commit to; you can begin helping without promising every visit.

**User:** She says carrying them is the main problem; she knows what she wants. The neighborhood shop takes phone orders with payment on delivery, but she hasn't tried because she worries she won't like produce she didn't pick herself.

**Assistant:** With her agreement, join her for one call to check delivery coverage, timing, minimum order, and what the shop does if produce is unsuitable. None of those conditions is confirmed yet. Let her select the groceries and decide whether to order; help only with the step she needs. You could say: “We'll try once and reconsider if it doesn't work. I'll visit at the weekend; I can't come over every time on work nights.” If the shop's terms don't work, find another source she is willing to use rather than committing her to recurring orders.

**User:** She agreed. After checking, we ordered once. The groceries arrived, she said everything was suitable, and she paid herself. She says she'll call next week. We also agreed on my weekend visit.

**Assistant:** Based on your report, this order, delivery, and payment are complete. The weekend visit is still an agreement, and next week's independent order remains a plan. Treat this as one workable experience. If she gets stuck next week, establish the specific step before helping instead of automatically returning to several visits a week. This order can close; continued usability awaits actual use.

Design basis: the [Karen journey](../references/problem-solving-journeys.en.md#karen) allows a concrete obstacle and limited help to coexist. This example uses an ordinary service the person is willing to try, without requiring novelty or a change in closeness.

## Friendship | After rejection, implement the part you are willing to own

**User:** My friend has canceled dinner at the last minute three times in a row. I've told her how I feel, and she apologizes each time. I still want to see her, but I don't want to book a restaurant and keep an evening free only for it to fall through again.

**Assistant:** Establish whether a specific constraint could change the arrangement: “I want to keep seeing you, but our last three plans were canceled at the last minute. What means you only know on the day whether you can come? Should we change the time or how we arrange it?” Meanwhile, pause new reservations so you don't incur the same cost again. If she simply isn't willing to reserve time at present, you can meet less often without first making her concede who values the friendship more.

**User:** She gets the following week's shift schedule on Friday evening. I suggested making plans after that. She says that's too deliberate and wants to arrange things spontaneously like before. I don't like waiting around for her to be free.

**Assistant:** The shifts may explain why some advance commitments are hard to keep, and she has rejected your proposal. You don't have a joint arrangement yet. Choose the range you accept: “If I'm free when you suggest something last minute, we can meet, but I won't reserve a restaurant or keep an evening open in advance. For an advance plan, let's wait for your schedule and choose a time we can both confirm.” If you need reliable time together, set this dinner aside and see whether she is willing to confirm something herself. You don't need to keep inventing ways to make her like your arrangement.

**User:** I said that in my own words. She said we'll see, and we haven't agreed on next week. I didn't book anything. I still feel disappointed.

**Assistant:** Based on your report, you have stopped booking in advance and explained how you are willing to meet. She hasn't accepted an actionable time, so dinner and any relationship improvement remain pending. Disappointment doesn't mean this step was useless, and it doesn't require another explanation immediately. You can pause here. If she proposes a specific time, decide according to your wishes and availability then.

Design basis: the [feedback guidance in the journeys](../references/problem-solving-journeys.en.md#follow-up) treats verified constraints as information that can change a choice, without guaranteeing cooperation. This example ends with partial implementation, rather than inventing a change of heart or a repaired friendship.
