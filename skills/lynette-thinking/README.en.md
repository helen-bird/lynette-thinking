# Using Lynette Thinking

[简体中文](README.md) · [How she chooses](references/decision-profile.en.md) · [Core instructions](SKILL.en.md)

**What would Lynette do?** Start with what you know: what do you want to change, and where are you stuck? This Skill uses what Lynette notices, wants to preserve, acts on, and corrects in specific situations to help you understand the problem, choose, and respond to what happens. You act as yourself.

## Start naturally

Workplace, family, and friendship dilemmas that require an action or a strategic conversation are in scope. You do not need to name the character. Automatic selection depends on your host and model. To specify it, write:

> Use $lynette-thinking to help me decide my next step. I already tried …, they responded …, and what I most want to change is …

A complete relationship history is unnecessary. Recent events, exact words, your goal, and previous attempts are useful starting points. Replies follow your language. All eight seasons may be cited; an explicit spoiler boundary for the current conversation is respected.

## What it distinguishes

| Where you are stuck | A distinction this perspective examines |
| --- | --- |
| Experience or ability is overlooked | Is the gap evidence of ability, a chance to participate, or formal eligibility? |
| You repeatedly get the costly role | Why are you specifically selected, and what commitments or rules does the other person recognize? |
| The original plan lacks resources | Which function is essential, and which sources have merely been excluded by convention? |
| You want help without losing control | Is the obstacle the resource, or the roles, contributions, and decision rights it creates? |
| You feel safer arranging things for others | Which burdens are real, and which judgments rely on an old relationship? Does new evidence require withdrawing interference? |

Specificity comes from these conditional tendencies and from [journeys from problem to outcome](references/problem-solving-journeys.en.md): how she investigates, communicates, acts, meets obstacles, and corrects herself. This is not a questionnaire. The assistant reads relevant material and a few cases on demand. Her mistakes remain so that cleverness is not equated with costless correctness.

## A useful start, then necessary information

You normally receive a manageable starting move: an action, a sentence to say when useful, why the other person might accept, and a different fallback. The assistant distinguishes facts from assumptions and asks for information most likely to change the decision. When someone else holds that information, it can help you decide whom to ask and how.

Initial clarification ordinarily asks one item per round, at most two when independently useful. The same decision receives no more than two proactive initial clarification rounds. Move to action when enough is known; a complete checklist is unnecessary. You can say “advise using the current facts,” “I do not know that,” or “stop asking questions.”

For time or money, distinguish personal and team resources, per-person and per-period shares, and totals without treating unknown shares as commitments. A later deadline still calls for preparing resources needed in the final execution window. Advice should fit the time, resources, and relationship costs you can bear now, reducing the step when useful.

## Return with what happened

You can continue with:

- “They cannot decide this; someone else has the authority.”
- “They agreed but missed the date we set.”
- “We tried it for two days, and my workload increased.”
- “That part is resolved; a different condition is blocking us now.”

The assistant first checks what happened and how far it remains from the original goal, then retains, adjusts, or changes the action. The two-round initial clarification limit does not end the process when you return with execution results. Later turns address only the most consequential current gap and continue through the next obstacle.

Starting a trial, receiving verbal agreement, and consistently achieving the goal are different stages. Closure checks the result against what you originally wanted to change: what has happened, what remains unresolved, and what still needs observation. Changes to the goal need your confirmation. An unresolved part remains explicit; temporary relief is not counted as resolution. You can also choose to pause or accept an explained tradeoff.

The [multi-turn examples](examples/guided-resolution.en.md) are designed demonstrations with fictional people and feedback. The [resolution guide](references/guided-resolution.en.md) explains how understanding, choice, action, feedback, and closure connect.

## Use the insight, examine the cost

[Decision episodes](references/decision-episodes.en.md) distinguish observed events, stated goals, interpretation, and consequences. She may take risks, seek control, or misread others. A real recommendation separately decides what to adopt, adapt, or reject.

[Contrasting examples](references/contrast-cases.en.md) show why a polite, reasonable answer may miss the causal turn, and when an analogy should be abandoned. Ordinary solutions are welcome when sufficient. You do not have to win everyone's approval.

Continuing depends on you bringing execution results back into the conversation. The Skill itself does not automatically retain memory across chats, schedule reminders, contact others, or execute actions for you, and cannot guarantee other people's responses or real-world results. For listening, say “Please just listen first.” For editing, say “Keep my decision; only improve the wording.” Character cases can expand a question, but do not replace specialized evidence for safety, medical, legal, or major financial decisions.
