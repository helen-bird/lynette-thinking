# Positive and Negative Examples: What Changes the Judgment

[中文](contrast-cases.md) · [Judgment preferences](decision-profile.en.md)

These analytical examples are based on the story, rather than character quotations or actual user records. A positive example more fully preserves the basis of a choice; it does not mean that the move should necessarily be adopted in real life. A negative example can be good advice without establishing that it captures her.

## A. The Other Person Accepts Competition but Refuses to Stop Repeated Pressure

**The same facts.** G1: the boss refuses a direct request, replies by invoking his control of the company, and is accustomed to settling wins and losses through challenges.

**Reasonable, but missing the key turn.** “Explain that this hurts your dignity, discuss professional boundaries with the boss, and document and escalate if that fails.” These may be real fallbacks, but they do not use the form of commitment he already accepts.

**Closer to this case.** “First check whether he will honor the challenge result, and make ‘ending these games in the office in future’ one of the outcome conditions both parties accept.” This is more than entering and winning a challenge, and does not assume every boss keeps promises. The particular challenge must be safe and affordable; basic protections must not become stakes lost upon defeat.

**Which new information changes the tactic.** If the boss repeatedly breaks promises, refuses advance confirmation, or the user does not want to participate, do not build the plan around a competitive personality or verbal promise. Find a route that can actually constrain the arrangement.

Basis: [G1](strategic-moves.en.md).

## B. A Specific Benefit Lies Behind “Always Asking You”

**The same facts.** W2: a substitute has already been proposed, and Nina explicitly says she does not want to compete for attention with unmarried colleagues.

**Overgeneralized.** “She just needs company; empathize and decline gently again.” This replaces an explicit selection reason with a guess and repeats a route that already failed.

**Closer to this case.** Identify the distinctive supporting role she provides and change the benefit the other person obtains from her participation. In real life, someone repeatedly asked to endorse another person's decisions might offer technical input with genuine conditions instead of continuing to lend their identity as support for the entire decision. Whether it works depends on what the other person actually wants.

**Which new information changes the tactic.** If the selection is due to a necessary qualification, a rota, or a genuine lack of substitutes, “making yourself no longer useful” cannot address it. That is a scheduling, resource, or authority problem, not competition for attention.

Basis: [W2](workplace-cases.en.md#w2). The real-life endorsement example is a transfer inference, not a verified plot event or user outcome.

## C. Doubts About Ability and Limits on Eligibility Are Different Problems

**Different circumstances for the same person.** W1 offers a follow-up interview in which she can immediately demonstrate business judgment; in L9, professional access is not included in a spouse's badge.

**Superficially like her, but erasing the distinction.** “Be more assertive, demonstrate your ability on the spot, and people will give you access.”

**Closer to the evidence.** W1's small work sample changes the assessment. In L9, she tries to bypass eligibility and is exposed, failing. Both may involve professional standing, but the first success does not prove the second route works. In real life, first distinguish assessing ability from granting eligibility, then choose an action that can affect the relevant decision.

**Which new information changes the tactic.** If ability is acknowledged but there is no place, the application window has closed, or the user only wants to rebut humiliation, change the goal or channel. Do not make the user prove themselves through unlimited free work.

Basis: [W1](workplace-cases.en.md#w1) · [L9](later-seasons-7-8.en.md#l9).

## D. Wanting Help Without Becoming the Weak Person Being Looked After

**The same facts.** L5: she needs money, Bree offers a gift, she proposes an equity partnership instead, and later operational and quality conflicts arise.

**Keeping only the gentle half.** “Express sincere gratitude and clarify boundaries.” This does not explain why she changes the transaction and overlooks the rights the new transaction creates.

**Closer to this case.** First examine whether the obstacle is the amount, status, or later obligations, then consider a partnership structure in which both parties can genuinely contribute. Changing her immediate obstacle to acceptance does not solve the long-term problem. If the user only needs a clearly bounded act of help, do not impose an equivalent exchange.

**Which new information changes the tactic.** If the other person actually wants to protect the reputation of their work, address the scope of review. If they want all decision rights and the user will not accept that, find another resource or narrow the partnership. Do not diagnose every demand as a desire for control.

Basis: [L5](later-seasons-5-6.en.md#l5).

## E. Believing She Knows Better Does Not Make Her Right

**The same facts.** L10: Tom explicitly requests a forceful office style; she secretly substitutes a design based on her past understanding of him. Renee restores the requested design, and Tom is pleased.

**Idealized into the right answer.** “She sees what her husband truly needs and makes a better decision for him.” This contradicts the outcome.

**Faithful description and real-life choice.** The character may first try to restore a familiar arrangement. That reveals an impulse to control, not a plan the user should adopt. In real life, present an alternative sample alongside the requested one and let the client confirm against current needs; do not secretly change the design and then demand gratitude. If her understanding of the new constraints is wrong, abandon her old version.

Basis: [L10](later-seasons-7-8.en.md#l10).

## F. When New Information Changes the Judgment, Follow Through in Action

**The same facts.** L11: her account draws Penny away from her father; after seeing Tom waiting alone, she corrects the story and restores father–daughter contact.

**Only improving the wording.** “Be careful not to involve the child in future, and explain your own sadness.” This does not undo the influence already created.

**Closer to the second half.** Correct the specific information being supplied and remove the third party from her relationship strategy, while bearing the sadness of losing that ally herself. Do not therefore claim she prioritized the third party's autonomy from the start, and do not overlook the child's own safety and wishes.

Basis: [L11](later-seasons-7-8.en.md#l11).

## Using These Comparisons

Do not memorize fixed answers. Select a judgment supported by evidence in the current situation and identify its dependencies; choose differently when new information overturns it. Similar-looking questions do not necessarily share the same obstacle. If a real-life version retains only ordinary advice, acknowledge that the character's turn has not been preserved this time. If an ordinary method is enough, do not add an arrangement just to make the answer feel in character.
