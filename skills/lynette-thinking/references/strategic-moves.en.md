## G1 | S2E14: Accept the Competitive Form, Change the Stakes

**The original impasse.** Tom bonds with Ed over their shared fraternity experience, but becomes trapped by humiliating wagers and nicknames. Lynette directly asks for them to stop; Ed refuses, saying that he runs the company.

**What she actually changed.** Rather than only debating whether the games should exist, she challenges Ed. They publicly agree that if she completes one challenge, office wagering will stop for a time. She eats a pound of raw bacon, the atmosphere among the spectators changes, and Ed agrees on the spot to stop; she then asks for a bucket. There is no evidence of long-term compliance.

**Mechanism.** She works through the competitive identity the boss accepts, placing a change he was unwilling to grant into outcome conditions both sides accept. One personal action is exchanged for a rule governing later interactions; the public commitment and spectators' perception of the cost also contribute to the change. It is not merely a joke or a more polite way to say no.

**Direction for generating options.** What form of assessment, experiment, process, or exchange does the other person already accept? Can one bounded action be linked to a specific later commitment? Establish the judgment method, return, and stopping condition first. Competition or strategically negotiating conditions need not be rewritten as an appeal for someone to admit fault.

**Conditions and costs.** The other person must be willing to recognize the agreement, and the user must have an action whose cost they can bear. Basic protections must not be wagered on winning or losing. For actual advice, choose a safe task while preserving the exchange structure. Do not copy the dangerous eating or present physical harm as a guaranteed demonstration of courage.

## G2 | S1E09: Bring Corporate Headhunting into Household Hiring

**The original impasse.** Waiting for a suitable nanny to apply is not working. The assumption that “a good candidate must currently be looking for work” limits the candidate pool.

**What she actually changed.** She applies corporate recruiting methods to finding a nanny, asks Bree where nannies working for wealthy families can be found, and actively approaches Claire, who is already employed. She offers a 20% pay increase and overtime pay. Claire sets limits on the role, including not running errands for the employer's friends, answering the phone, or doing laundry, and they agree on employment.

**Mechanism.** She transfers an established method from another field to expand the supply pool, then replaces a wage-only offer with a combination of compensation and task scope. She does not merely urge acquaintances to introduce more candidates.

**Direction for generating options.** Which resources has the user excluded because of who currently has them, industry conventions, or the recruiting method? Where are these people actually found, and why might they choose a different arrangement? What genuine new combination can be offered, rather than simply asking them to help?

**Conditions and costs.** Candidates can decline, and the budget and scope must be real. The story improves conditions with money; that does not prove everyone can offer more pay. In other problems, look for time, content, or convenience that can actually be exchanged. An immediate hire does not prove long-term stability, and Claire's later departure must not be ignored.

## W2 | Change “Why Do They Choose Me in Particular?”

**The original impasse.** Nina asks Lynette to accompany her to bars and links refusal to project assignments. She does not want another unmarried colleague as a substitute, because the married Lynette will not compete for men's attention.

**What she actually changed.** Lynette changes how she participates socially, attracting attention in the bar so she no longer supplies the backdrop Nina wanted. The narration presents her as escaping the bar outings; this cannot be generalized into a claim that no boss would retaliate.

**Mechanism and direction for generating options.** The other person may keep choosing the user not only because the user agrees, but because they provide a particular convenience or position. Identify that condition, then change the form of provision, structure of participation, or alternative source so the most advantageous original choice changes. Simply explaining again that you are tired has not changed the other person's benefit.

**Conditions and costs.** This does not mean deliberately doing poor work, pretending to be incapable, or manufacturing losses. Competition in voluntary adult social life need not automatically be moralized, but assess the actual costs and later relationship; do not assume the other person will withdraw as expected.

## W6 | Turn Existing Objects and Behaviors into a New Channel

**The original impasse.** Advertising for a cleaning product is still looking for exposure through familiar placements.

**What she actually changed.** Lynette proposes printing advertisements on dry-cleaning bags: the target consumers already encounter them and keep the bags in their closets. Those present approve and discuss a chain-based channel; actual placement or conversion is not shown.

**Mechanism and direction for generating options.** Redefine what can carry information. Follow what the target audience already does to find necessary steps, objects they encounter or keep, or unused attention, so the new goal can travel along an existing route. Identify the specific carrier, why the user would see it, and who provides the point of access. “Understand the user better” does not count as generating a channel.

**Conditions and costs.** Check the audience, partners, costs, and reach; do not assume seeing something leads to action. Taking over Tom's chance to speak causes a relationship conflict, but that should not erase the design of the advertising medium.

## W5 | Turn a Large Resource Request into the Last Trigger Condition

**The original impasse.** Childcare is needed. The company already has an arrangement, but it cannot operate because too few people have joined.

**What she actually changed.** Lynette finds the threshold of 16 participants and discovers that Ed's daughter is the missing participant. After her first attempt to persuade Fran fails, she addresses Fran's concerns about her identity as a mother. Childcare is operating at the end of the episode.

**Mechanism and direction for generating options.** Instead of requesting an entirely new set of resources, find the missing condition in a system that already exists but has not been activated. Who can fill the final gap, and why are they unwilling? Would satisfying that condition actually unlock the whole resource? Do not merely say “seek more support.”

**Conditions and costs.** The threshold must be real, and the key person's willingness cannot be assumed. Her instigation and holding onto the child escalate the conflict in the story. A transferable approach need not manufacture a crisis; preserve the work on the threshold and identity concerns.

## L1 | Let Go of a Complete Matching Set, Preserve the Function, and Combine Small Resources

**The original impasse.** On opening day, an ordering mistake produces 50 toddler high chairs, and there is no time to restore the original plan.

**What she actually changed.** She accepts a source that can provide only five chairs, sends staff to search, and combines different chairs into usable seats that evening. The restaurant opens with mismatched chairs.

**Mechanism and direction for generating options.** Failure of the original plan does not mean the function cannot be delivered. Separate non-negotiable functions from customary forms, and seek a combination of incomplete sources rather than insisting on one perfect replacement. Establish the gap, timing, and actual effect of the combination.

**Conditions and costs.** The user cannot unilaterally cancel client commitments, safety, or necessary quality. Specify who collects, transports, and returns items, so executing the creative recovery is not all handed back to the user. The story establishes only that the restaurant opened that evening, not long-term profitability.

## W1 / F4 | Let a Real Small Sample or Symmetrical Comparison Change the Judgment

**What she actually changed.** In W1, facing doubts about her identity as a mother and Ed's imminent flight, Lynette directly identifies award opportunities and website problems at the company, and is hired on the spot. She turns “believe in my ability” into a short piece of work that has already happened. In F4, after apologizing for excessive accusations, she exchanges the relationship positions by kissing Carlos, making Gaby feel the same kind of boundary crossing; it also causes Carlos to misunderstand.

**Mechanism and direction for generating options.** If someone does not believe in an ability, provide a concrete sample. If a standard applies only to one side, test the same rule in symmetrical positions. Specify what the demonstration preserves, what it exchanges, and how the outcome will be observed. This is not merely “put yourself in their shoes” or proving effort without limit.

**Conditions and costs.** A work sample should be real and bounded; a comparison should be low-cost, informed, and allow people to leave. Another person's body or relationship cannot secretly be used as a demonstration tool. Change that execution method while retaining an observable symmetrical structure, rather than returning to a request for polite expression alone.

## F2 / L5 | Redesign the Exchange So Help Can Be Accepted

**What she actually changed.** In F2, she discovers Karen's specific difficulty opening a medication bottle, promises limited assistance, accepts the lamp again, and proposes occasional mutual help. The relationship moves from potentially endless companionship to a specific obstacle and an arrangement in which both can contribute. In L5, she proposes replacing Bree's $20,000 gift with a 15% stake in the restaurant, turning one-way assistance into a partnership; later authority conflicts and liquidation also actually occur.

**Mechanism and direction for generating options.** What is rejected may be the identity or obligation implied by accepting a resource, rather than the resource itself. Recombine the real contents of the exchange: who contributes, retains, and receives what? Dignity need not depend on pretending not to need help, and reciprocity need not depend on inventing needs.

**Conditions and costs.** A new label is not a solution by itself: a new transaction creates new rights, obligations, and exit costs. Look first at the smallest real contribution, without assuming money, equity, or unlimited effort from the other person is necessary.

## How to Use the Remaining Cases

The old cases remain valuable, but not all show her inventing a successful move. W3/F5/L9/L10 mainly test for counter-moves, evidence, qualifications, or misjudgment. F1/L2/L6/L8/L11/L12 often help with goals, burdens, support, and repair. The new housing arrangement in L3, the key phone call in L4, and the well-received office decoration in L10 mainly come from other people and cannot serve as evidence that she invented a solution. Training a replacement in L7 offers an angle on creating future options, but whether that resource was authorized for use remained unresolved.

In ordinary answers, use generative cases to find an action first, then read these materials according to the specific risks. Do not spread the warnings from every case evenly across the body of the answer.
